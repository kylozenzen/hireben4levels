03 — A day on the queue

What Tuesday looks like.

Strategy decks are easy to write and hard to verify. A daily rhythm isn't. Here's the shape of a normal day — the thing that determines whether comments get answered, DMs get routed, and tomorrow's post is ready before tomorrow.

Roughly half the day is responding. That's the job, and the calendar should admit it.

7:15 AM

Overnight scan

Comments, DMs, mentions, tags, anything that happened while we slept. Two things get flagged before I've finished coffee: whatever needs a human today, and whatever the team needs to know about.

8:30 AM

Publish and verify

Post goes out and then gets checked like it might be wrong — caption formatting, tags, cover frame, link, first comment, how it actually renders on a phone. Publishing isn't done when it's scheduled, it's done when it's confirmed.

9:00 AM

First response window

Every comment from the last 24 hours gets a reply or an explicit decision not to reply. Early replies are the cheapest distribution lever available and most brands skip them.

11:00 AM

Inbox

DMs triaged and routed: customer service, wholesale, creator, partnership, press. Each one logged with an owner and a next action. Nothing sits unread overnight.

1:00 PM

Paid comment sweep

Ad comment sections get the same voice as organic — answered, not ignored. Recurring objections go into a log that Creative and Performance see weekly, because the ad comments are the cheapest customer research in the building.

2:30 PM

Listening hour

Creators, competitors, formats, the deal and warehouse communities, the protein discourse of the week. Output is three candidates: conversations we could credibly enter, and the reason for each.

4:00 PM

Tomorrow, confirmed

Assets in hand, copy written, approvals chased today rather than at 8am tomorrow. If something's missing, it gets raised now while people are still online.

5:00 PM

Close the loop

Log what performed, what got asked repeatedly, which creators worth a follow-up appeared, and what needs to reach Brand, Creative, or CX. A day that isn't logged is a day the rest of the team can't use.

FRIDAY

"What we saw" note

One page to the wider team: five observations, three ideas we could execute inside a week, one conversation we deliberately stayed out of and why. Short enough that people read it.

05 — The response system

Volume needs a system, not stamina.

Anyone can answer comments for a week. Doing it for a year without dropping the important ones takes rules written down before you're busy. Here's what I'd put on paper in the first two weeks.

Four decisions, written down before we're busy — so anyone covering can make them too.

Four decisions, every time

  • Answer. Product questions, taste, dosing, where to buy, jokes worth playing along with. In brand voice, specific, never copy-pasted.
  • Route. Orders, refunds, wholesale, press, partnerships. Handed to the right team with context, then tracked until it's actually closed.
  • Escalate. Anything touching health claims, adverse reactions, legal, or a customer who is genuinely upset in public. Fast, to a named person, no improvising.
  • Leave it. Bait, brand fights, politics, conversations where a supplement company has nothing true to add. Knowing when not to post is part of brand voice.

Windows and guardrails

  • First four hours matter most. New posts get monitored actively while the algorithm is deciding; replies inside the first hour are distribution, not politeness.
  • DMs answered same business day, every day, with a triage pass first thing and a second before close.
  • Paid comments swept twice daily. Same standard as organic — a customer doesn't know or care which one they commented on.
  • A one-page voice doc. What we joke about, what we never claim, how we handle competitor mentions and health questions. So anyone covering for me sounds like us.
  • An objection log. Every recurring "but does it…" goes in it, and the top five ship to Creative and Performance weekly. Ad comments are free customer research.

What that sounds like

Rules are abstract, so here are three replies as I'd actually write them — including the one I wouldn't send.

Answer · Instagram comment

is this sweetened with stevia? every stevia protein I've tried tastes like chemicals

Stevia leaf extract and monk fruit, and that's the whole sweetener list — no sucralose, no added sugar. The chemical aftertaste you're describing usually comes from stevia doing all the work alone. Grab a single serving before you commit to the bag if you're unsure; that's a fair test.

Why: answers the actual question, names the ingredients instead of deflecting, and offers a low-risk way in. No superlatives.

Route + repair · DM

ordered 10 days ago and it still hasn't shipped. no reply to two emails. cool company 👍

That's a fair amount of frustration and I'd feel the same. Send me your order number here and I'll walk it over to our support team myself rather than pointing you back at the inbox that's already ignored you twice. I'll follow up here either way so it doesn't disappear again.

Why: takes ownership without over-apologizing or making promises support hasn't agreed to. The follow-up commitment is the part most brands skip — and then it goes in the tracker.

Leave it · TikTok comment

[competitor] just got exposed for heavy metals lmaooo y'all should post about it

No reply sent

Why: dunking on a competitor over a safety story is a short-term win and a long-term liability. It goes in the listening log instead — and if the category conversation grows, we answer it with our own label, not their bad week.

06 — Listening, and what it produces

Listening is only useful if it turns into something.

The listing is careful to say I wouldn't own strategy but would be expected to have opinions. Fair. Here's what I'd watch, the filter I'd run it through, and the kind of ideas that come out the other side.

Most of what gets watched should not become a post. The filter is the valuable part.

The daily watch

  • Creators in protein, fitness, food and "healthy-ish" lifestyle — including the ones already posting us unprompted.
  • Competitor comment sections, which are more honest than any survey and cost nothing to read.
  • Warehouse and deal communities — Costco subreddits, deal sites, the people who find things on shelves before we announce them.
  • Format shifts, not just trends. What's working structurally on TikTok this month versus last.
  • The ingredient discourse, which moves fast and occasionally turns into a scare the whole category has to answer.

The filter

  • Can we say something true here? If participating requires exaggerating, we skip it — that's the brand's whole premise.
  • Can we do it in 24 hours? A trend we ship next week isn't a trend, it's a memo.
  • Would a customer care, or only a marketer? The second one is how brands end up posting about themselves.
  • Does it survive the comments? If the top reply is going to be a fair criticism we can't answer, that's a signal.

Six ideas it would produce

Series

Six Ingredients

One ingredient a week, read off the actual bag, explained in plain language — what it does, why it's in there, what we could have used instead and didn't. Boring on paper, extremely effective in a category where everyone's suspicious.

Series

The same five comment questions, answered properly on video by whoever actually knows — sourcing, sweeteners, texture, bloating. Then pinned, reused, and linked in DMs forever.

The flavor that didn't make it, the additive that would have improved texture, the claim legal wouldn't let us make and shouldn't have. "Nothing fake" is more convincing as a story about restraint than as a slogan.

07 — Creator coordination

The ones you lose are the ones nobody logged.

Creator inbound arrives in the same inbox as everything else, which is exactly how promising people get missed. The fix isn't enthusiasm, it's a tracker and a response standard.

Inbound

  • Everyone gets an answer. Even a no. Creators talk to each other, and a brand with a reputation for ghosting pays for it later.
  • A simple triage rubric — audience fit, content quality, whether they've ever mentioned the category, whether they'd have used us anyway.
  • Gifting runs as a workflow, not a favor: request, approve, ship, expect, log. With dates attached so follow-up isn't guesswork.
  • Every conversation has a next action and an owner. That's the whole trick.

Outbound and handoff

  • An advocate radar. A weekly short list of people already posting us unprompted — the cheapest partnerships available, and they go straight to Influencer Marketing.
  • Context in the handoff. When a relationship moves to the Influencer team, it moves with history, not a name and a link.
  • Records that survive me. Conversations, agreements, product sent, content received, usage rights and expiry — in one place anyone can read.
  • Consistent follow-through. The difference between a creator ecosystem and a list of creators is whether anyone circles back.

08 — Systems I'd build

The part nobody puts in a portfolio.

Your listing asks for someone who makes the operation "increasingly organized, scalable, and self-sufficient." That's a systems ask, not a content ask. These are the five I'd build first.

System 01

One calendar, with status

Every item has an owner, an asset, a status, and a publish time. Not a doc that describes intentions — a board where anyone can see what's shipping tomorrow and what's blocked today.

System 02

A publishing checklist

Caption, tags, cover frame, alt text, link, first comment, cross-post variants. Boring, written down, and the reason a post never goes out with the wrong link again.

System 03

Four trackers that stay current

DMs and follow-ups, creator conversations, recurring objections, and the asset library with usage rights and expiry dates. Current beats comprehensive.

System 04

Vendor and finance discipline

Onboarding, invoices, W-9s, the paperwork behind creator and freelance work. It's in your listing and most applicants will skip past it — I'd rather be the person who keeps finance from chasing me.

System 05

Visibility on purpose

A standing weekly post to the team: what shipped, what's next, what needs a decision from someone else. Half the value of an operator is that nobody has to ask for a status update.

09 — What I'd report

Reports that end in a decision.

I've never been able to treat social as a scoreboard. A post that does big reach and moves nobody toward a first bag is a post I'd want to change. So the reporting is built to change next week's work, not to prove last week's.

The chart is never the report. The sentence underneath it is.

Weekly, one page

  • Reach, saves and sends, net follower change — with saves and sends leading, because they predict what travels.
  • Top three and bottom three posts, each with a one-line why. The why is the entire point of the exercise.
  • Response health: comment and DM volume, time to first reply, what got routed where.
  • The five questions people asked most — which is next week's content, already written by customers.

Monthly, deeper

  • Patterns by format and hook, not by individual post. Repeat what compounds, kill what only worked once.
  • Objections and sentiment shipped to Creative and Performance so ads answer real hesitations.
  • Creator signal: who's posting us organically, who followed through, who's worth a real partnership.
  • One recommendation for what to stop doing. A report that never removes anything isn't a report, it's a scoreboard.
