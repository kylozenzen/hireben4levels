05 — How I'd run it

The operating system, not just the calendar.

Your listing asks for someone who makes this "increasingly organized, scalable, and self-sufficient." That's a systems ask, not a content ask — and roughly half of any given day here is response, not publishing. Here's the rhythm and the rules underneath it.

Roughly half the day is responding. That's the job, and the calendar should admit it.

  1. Own the daily rhythm. Overnight scan first thing, then publish-and-verify — a post isn't done when it's scheduled, it's done when the caption, tags, cover frame and link are confirmed. First response window inside the hour, DM inbox triaged with an owner and next action on everything, a paid-comment sweep held to the same standard as organic, a listening hour, and next-day assets confirmed before people go home rather than chased at 8am.
  2. Own the response system. Every comment and DM sorts into one of four decisions, written down before we're busy: answer (product, taste, where to buy — in brand voice, never copy-pasted), route (orders, refunds, wholesale, press — tracked to closed), escalate (health claims, adverse reactions, legal — fast, to a named person), or leave it (bait, brand fights, anything we'd have to exaggerate to join). A one-page voice doc means anyone covering for me sounds like us.
  3. Own the listening pipeline. A daily watch — creators, competitor comment sections, warehouse and deal communities, format shifts, the ingredient discourse — filtered through four questions before anything ships: can we say something true here, can we do it in 24 hours, would a customer care or only a marketer, does it survive the comments. Most of what gets watched should not become a post; the filter is the valuable part.
  4. Own the systems that scale it. One calendar with status, not a doc describing intentions. A publishing checklist so nothing goes out with the wrong link. Four trackers that stay current — DMs, creator conversations, recurring objections, the asset library with usage rights and expiry. Vendor and finance discipline on the paperwork behind creator and freelance work, because most applicants skip past it.
  5. Own reporting that ends in a decision. Weekly: reach, saves and sends, top and bottom three posts with a one-line why, response health, and the five questions people asked most — next week's content, already written by customers. Monthly: patterns by format and hook, objections shipped to Creative and Performance, creator signal, and one recommendation for what to stop. The chart is never the report. The sentence underneath it is.

The ones you lose are the ones nobody logged.

That's one half of "creators" — the inbound kind, below. The other half is closer to home: Levels is also hiring a dedicated content creator, and from what came up in the interview, Lauren — Strategy Director for Brand & Social — currently owns both strategy and a lot of the execution herself, and this role exists to take the execution part off her plate so she can stay focused on brand and direction.

From the interview

The read I'd bring to that structure: take Lauren's creative brief, turn it into a shot list and a working brief the content creator can actually shoot from, run point on execution day to day, and close the loop with what shipped and how it performed — so the strategy stays hers, the execution stops being a bottleneck she has to carry personally, and she's not relitigating the same brief twice.

And separately — the creator-partnership motion this role would also own:

Inbound

  • Everyone gets an answer. Even a no. Creators talk to each other, and a brand with a reputation for ghosting pays for it later.
  • A simple triage rubric — audience fit, content quality, whether they've ever mentioned the category, whether they'd have used us anyway.
  • Gifting runs as a workflow, not a favor: request, approve, ship, expect, log. With dates attached so follow-up isn't guesswork.
  • Every conversation has a next action and an owner. That's the whole trick.

Outbound and handoff

  • An advocate radar. A weekly short list of people already posting us unprompted — the cheapest partnerships available, and they go straight to Influencer Marketing.
  • Context in the handoff. When a relationship moves to the Influencer team, it moves with history, not a name and a link.
  • Records that survive me. Conversations, agreements, product sent, content received, usage rights and expiry — in one place anyone can read.
  • Consistent follow-through. The difference between a creator ecosystem and a list of creators is whether anyone circles back.
